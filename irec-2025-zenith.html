Competition Overview

IREC (Intercollegiate Rocket Engineering Competition) is the world's largest student rocketry competition, bringing together 140+ teams from universities across the globe. Teams design, build, and launch high-powered rockets targeting specific altitude goals while competing in technical excellence categories.

At IREC 2025, Monash HPR's Project Zenith won the prestigious Jim Furfaro Award for Technical Excellence and placed 2nd in our category. I served as Avionics Vice Lead responsible for launch operations, system integration, and the final "ready to run" sign-off before engine ignition.

My Role: Avionics Vice Lead - Launch Readiness

As Avionics Vice Lead, I represented the 5-person avionics team in technical meetings and owned propulsion control integration and test readiness. I was the final authority who signed off "ready to run" before any test or launch.

Pre-Launch Verification Checklist

Electrical Continuity

Verified all harness connections, checked for shorts or open circuits, confirmed power distribution to all subsystems.

Sensor Sanity Checks

Zeroed pressure transducers, verified thermocouple readings, checked load cell drift, validated sensor plausibility ranges.

Communications Health

Brought up and verified stability of CAN bus, RS-422 serial links, and MQTT telemetry. Monitored for dropped packets or timeouts.

State Machine Dry Runs

Executed complete fill/ignite/abort sequences without propellant to verify software logic, timing, and interlocks.

🔑 Why This Matters: My verification determined whether the engine was safe to run. Lives and expensive hardware depended on catching issues before ignition—there's no room for "probably fine" when dealing with 4 kN of thrust and cryogenic oxidizers.

Crisis Management: Pre-Launch Sensor Failure

The night before our scheduled launch window, our capacitive propellant sensor—critical for verifying fill levels—started giving erratic readings. This was a potential show-stopper that could scrub our launch.

The Problem

During final pad setup checks, the sensor showed noise spikes and inconsistent measurements. We'd never seen this behavior in testing. With limited time and tools in the field, we had to diagnose and fix the issue before morning.

Root Cause Analysis

Step 1: Isolate Variables

Swapped cables, checked power supply stability, verified firmware hadn't corrupted. Problem persisted—pointed to electrical noise.

Step 2: Trace Signal Path

Used oscilloscope to examine sensor output. Saw high-frequency noise coupled into signal lines—likely from nearby motor controllers or RF interference.

Step 3: Field Fix

Implemented additional shielding on sensor cables, rerouted away from high-current lines, added ferrite beads. Measurements stabilized.

Step 4: Validation

Ran repeated fill simulations overnight. Sensor held calibration, readings were repeatable. Cleared for launch.

Outcome: System performed flawlessly during launch operations. The sensor provided accurate fill-level readings, enabling safe propellant loading and successful ignition.

💡 Lesson Learned: This experience taught me the importance of environmental testing. Our lab tests didn't replicate the electromagnetic environment of the launch pad. Now I always ask: "What's different about the deployment environment?"

Pre-Launch System Rebuild Decision

During hot-fire testing leading up to IREC, we experienced compounding electrical issues: intermittent connectors, unreliable pyro channels, and inconsistent actuator behavior. Each issue was fixable individually, but the pattern suggested deeper problems.

The Call

A propulsion teammate challenged our "incremental fixes" approach. After reviewing test data and failure modes together, I agreed—a complete electrical system rebuild before flight would be safer than hoping individual patches held.

Execution Under Pressure

We had days, not weeks. I mobilized 20+ teammates across subsections to execute the overhaul:

  • Replaced all connectors with higher-reliability crimped assemblies
  • Rewired pyro channels with redundant paths and improved shielding
  • Redesigned actuator wiring harness with strain relief and vibration protection
  • Implemented comprehensive continuity testing procedures
  • Validated entire system with end-to-end checkout before travel

Result: The rebuilt system performed nominally during launch. No electrical faults, no anomalies. The decision to rebuild—and the team's execution—directly enabled our successful flight.

🎯 Leadership Insight: This taught me that being a technical lead means knowing when to push back on "good enough" and advocate for doing it right, even when timelines are tight. It also showed me the power of trusting your team—when you give people clear objectives and empower them to execute, they rise to the challenge.

Key Takeaways

Test Readiness is a Discipline

The "ready to run" checklist wasn't bureaucracy—it was the difference between a successful launch and a catastrophic failure. Disciplined pre-test verification saves hardware and lives.

Field Engineering Under Pressure

You can't debug with printf statements when you're on a launch pad with limited tools and time. Systematic troubleshooting, environmental awareness, and creative problem-solving are critical skills.

Know When to Rebuild vs. Patch

Incremental fixes can mask systemic problems. Sometimes the right answer is to tear it down and build it right, even if it's painful short-term.

Technical Communication Wins Awards

Our presentation clearly explained complex systems to judges with varying backgrounds. Being able to communicate technical work to non-specialists is as important as the engineering itself.
